# Biomechanics of Muslim Prayer (Salat)

Source: Nazish N, Kalra N. Muslim prayer - a new form of physical activity: a narrative review. International Journal of Health Sciences and Research. 2018;8(7):337-344.

## Overview

Salat is performed by Muslim worshippers five times a day and is a physical as well as a spiritual act involving total obedience and submission to Allah. The procedure includes standing, bowing, prostration, and sitting. These movements activate nearly all muscles of the human body without muscle fatigue and induce serenity on body and soul.

Joints involved in prayer include: shoulders, wrists, elbows, metatarsophalangeal joints, proximal and distal interphalangeal joints, temporomandibular joint, jaw joint, spinal cord, pelvis, knees, ankles, subtalar joints, and atlantoaxial joints.

## Standing (Qiyam)

Starting position is standing with the center of pressure midway between the insteps of the two feet. Worshippers raise their hands up to the earlobes and bring them down, holding the left wrist with the right hand on the abdomen, above the navel, or on the chest. During voluntary clenching of the right hand, blood flow is increased in the hand area of the left motor cortex and corresponding sensory areas in the post-central gyrus.

**Joint involvement:** ankles, knees, hips, spine, shoulders, elbows, wrists.
**Muscle activation:** quadriceps, calf muscles, core stabilizers, shoulder abductors.

## Bowing (Ruku)

Bowing is done by forward movement of the vertebral column, resulting in activation of the back muscles especially the paraspinal muscles. The movement is supported by two straight hands grasping the hyperextended knees. This requires near 90-degree forward hip flexion while maintaining a neutral lumbar spine.

**Joint involvement:** hips, knees, spine, shoulders, wrists, elbows.
**Muscle activation:** paraspinal muscles (erector spinae), hamstrings, gastrocnemius (medial and lateral), quadriceps, shoulder flexors.

**Adaptations for injury:** If a person has active disc herniation, rounding the lower back during ruku significantly increases intradiscal pressure. Focus on "hinging at the hips" by pushing the glutes back, keeping the chest up and spine flat. Only bend as far as possible before pain is triggered, or perform seated.

## Prostration (Sujud)

Prostration is done from the standing position to kneeling, putting the head down and touching the ground with the forehead. The palms remain parallel to the ears, with flexed elbows touching the ground. Proper sujud requires distributing body weight across seven points of contact: forehead, nose, two palms, two knees, and pads of two feet.

**Joint involvement:** wrists, elbows, shoulders, knees, ankles, spine, neck, metatarsophalangeal joints.
**Muscle activation:** triceps brachii, shoulder stabilizers (rotator cuff), wrist flexors, neck flexors, core muscles.

**Adaptations for injury:** A beginner who cannot get their palms to the ground effectively may be rounding their back excessively. Improper hand placement (too narrow) can put undue strain on the shoulder joint. Wrist extension in standard sujud reaches 70-90 degrees. Post-fracture or carpal tunnel syndrome may make this painful. Using a fist position (weight on knuckles) or padded supports reduces wrist extension load.

## Sitting (Julus)

Sitting is done on the left leg with knee flexed, inverted dorsi-flexed ankle, and flexed right knee and metatarsophalangeal joint. This deep squatting position requires maximal knee flexion with shins lying parallel on the ground.

**Joint involvement:** knees, ankles, hips, metatarsophalangeal joints.
**Muscle activation:** quadriceps, hip flexors, ankle dorsiflexors.

## Neck Biomechanics in Prayer

The neck is rotated during salam (turning right and left to wish peace). EMG studies show significant activation of neck extensors and sternocleidomastoid muscles during this movement. Cervical spondylosis may be prevented by regular salat practice due to controlled neck rotation. The head is lower than the heart during sujud, increasing blood flow to the brain.

**Adaptations for injury:** If neck pain prevents standard head position, adjust head alignment to avoid pain. Tuck the chin slightly and avoid excessive cervical rotation during ruku and sujud.

## Shoulder Biomechanics in Prayer

During takbir, the hands are raised to the earlobes, requiring shoulder abduction and external rotation. During ruku, the hands grasp the knees, requiring shoulder flexion and internal rotation. During sujud, the palms are placed parallel to the ears, requiring shoulder extension and scapular stabilization.

EMG studies show significant upper trapezius activation during salam position. Shoulder problems may arise from improper hand placement during sujud (too narrow) causing undue strain on the shoulder joint. Post-surgical shoulders (rotator cuff repair, arthroplasty) may lack range of motion to place hands on knees during ruku or palms flat during sujud.

**Adaptations for injury:** If shoulder injury prevents placing hands on knees during ruku, bend as far as safely possible. The pillar is the bowing motion with intention, not the hand placement. If the shoulder cannot bear weight in sujud, use a raised support or chair.

## Arm Biomechanics in Prayer

The arm (upper limb) extends from the shoulder to the hand, comprising the upper arm, elbow, forearm, and wrist. During ruku, the arms extend to grasp the knees. During sujud, the forearms support body weight with elbows flexed. Post-surgical arms or arm injuries may prevent these positions.

**Adaptations for injury:** If arm pain or injury prevents extending the arms to grasp the knees during ruku, bend only as far as safely possible. The pillar is the bowing motion with intention, not the hand placement. If arm pain prevents bearing weight on the forearms during sujud, use a raised support or chair.

## Wrist and Hand Biomechanics in Prayer

Sujud distributes approximately 30-40 percent of body weight through the wrists. Wrist extension in standard sujud reaches 70-90 degrees. Post-fracture or carpal tunnel syndrome may make palm placement painful.

**Adaptations for injury:** Using a fist position (weight on knuckles) or padded supports reduces wrist extension load. The worshipper may also use forearms or a padded support for weight bearing.

## Elbow Biomechanics in Prayer

The elbow is flexed during sujud, with the forearms supporting body weight. Elbow extension is required during ruku to reach the knees. Severe arthritis or post-surgical elbows with flexion contractures may prevent full extension.

**Adaptations for injury:** If elbow flexion contracture prevents reaching the knees, bend to maximum safe capacity. The intention and effort to bow fulfill the obligation.

## Ankle and Foot Biomechanics in Prayer

The ankles are plantarflexed in sujud, with the tops of the feet on the ground. During sitting (julus), the ankle is dorsiflexed with the left leg inverted. Acute ankle sprain or post-operative immobilization may prevent these positions.

**Adaptations for injury:** A rolled towel under the ankles reduces dorsiflexion demand. If ankle injury prevents the tops of the feet from touching the ground, the worshipper may use socks, a soft mat, or sit in a chair.

## Spine and Lower Back Biomechanics in Prayer

Ruku requires forward flexion of the vertebral column, activating paraspinal muscles. Sujud requires spinal flexion with the head lower than the heart. Studies show Muslim prayer movements have similar effects to specific lower back muscle exercises.

**Adaptations for injury:** To reduce pain from lumbar herniation, focus on "hinging at the hips" by pushing the glutes straight back, keeping the chest up and spine flat. Do not round the upper or lower back. If this cue is insufficient, only bend as far as possible before pain is triggered, or perform the movement seated.

## Hip Biomechanics in Prayer

Ruku requires near 90-degree hip flexion. Deep squatting during julus requires maximal knee flexion with hip flexion. Studies show Muslim patients with knee osteoarthritis have better range of flexion due to regular prayer practice.

**Adaptations for injury:** Hip replacement patients may need chair modifications. If hip immobility prevents standard prayer postures, sitting on a chair is permitted with torso inclined forward.

## Knee Biomechanics in Prayer

Ruku requires knee extension to support the bowing position. Sujud requires knee flexion with shins on the ground. Deep squatting during julus maximally flexes the knees. Studies show regular namaz is associated with improved joint health and better range of motion.

**Adaptations for injury:** If knee pain prevents kneeling, the worshipper may sit on a chair and incline forward. If a recent surgical procedure prevents the knees from touching the ground, sitting on a chair with deeper incline for sujud than for ruku is permitted.

## Leg and Lower Limb Biomechanics in Prayer

Qiyam (standing) requires sustained quadriceps and calf activation. Lower extremity weakness or pain may make prolonged standing difficult. Weight shifting or a support frame reduces single-leg load.

**Adaptations for injury:** If leg pain or weakness prevents standing, the worshipper should sit. If sitting is also difficult, they may lie on their side. The principle is to perform prayer in the manner closest to the standard form that the body can sustain without harm.

## Rehabilitation Applications

Salat has been proposed as a feasible low-impact therapeutic exercise for geriatric and disabled patients. The movements provide concentric and eccentric muscle actions which play an important role in postural stability and balance. Studies show improved balance in elderly populations who regularly perform salat postures.

For patients with advanced degenerative arthritis of the knee, movements of the affected joint are often preserved because of the traditional way of sitting and praying, which forces the knees into deep flexion for long periods many times a day.
